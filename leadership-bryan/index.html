Good Leadership
Coheres.
Bad Leadership Fractures.

01

The Problem

There is a considerable lack of truly productive leadership outside of traditional hierarchical military systems.

In the military: roughly 50% of commanders are good to excellent. In the corporate world since then? So few and far between that no name readily comes to mind.

The Gap
Military Good Commanders ~50%
Corporate Good Leaders Rare
Day-to-Day Interactions Severely Lacking

"Unless there's an innate kernel of ability in that individual, it really comes down to your fundamentals. How do you inspire and influence people to do their best work—and do so in a way that isn't demeaning?"

02

Two Types of Leaders

"There are leaders of men, and there are leaders of leaders."

— First Sergeant, U.S. Army

I Direct Leadership

Leaders of Men

Get in the trenches. Spit out a stirring speech and rouse the passions of the troops. Direct influence, direct impact.

Characteristics
  • • Visible presence in the field
  • • Emotional rallying capability
  • • Individual connection mastery
II Strategic Leadership

Leaders of Leaders

Steadfast. Guide the folks who are making the decisions. Create the conditions for others to lead well.

Characteristics
  • • Strategic consistency
  • • Decision-maker guidance
  • • Systemic influence

Both are essential. The distinction matters for knowing which mode you're operating in.

03

The Fundamentals

Leadership reduces to fundamentals—principles that transcend context, hierarchy, and organizational type.

01

Sincerity

"People can sniff out insincerity in two seconds." Being sincere and well-intentioned is the foundation everything else is built on.

02

Context Over Binary

"I don't believe in binary for most problems." When a leader takes time to understand context before making a decision, it resonates with people.

03

Emotional Quotient

Knowing your own feelings and emotions, reading others, and responding in a way that's constructive. The EQ that separates adequate from excellent.

04

Praise Public, Admonish Private

A principle so fundamental it was passed down as a family value. Protect dignity while maintaining accountability.

05

Model First

"Modeling behavior is the first way to do things." You yourself have to be able to do it. Lead yourself before you can lead others.

06

Shared Sacrifice

Understanding the sacrifice folks make—and being willing to sacrifice with them. This flattens hierarchy. It's less class-based, more shared purpose.

04

The Hardest Thing

Getting people to treat the unit as their own.

Ownership. It's probably the hardest thing. Getting folks to buy into the unit as if it's their own and treat it that way.

The Tangible

"I don't want to walk by trash in my parking lot—because it's my unit, it's my parking lot too."

The Behavioral

"Watching someone else's behavior and saying: 'Hey, man, that's not how we do things around here.'"

The Insight

"If you look at high-performing units, there is generally an internal sense of standard that they apply laterally. It doesn't come from the top down."

05

The Development Framework

Three distinct approaches to developing people—each temporal, each with its purpose.

Past

Counseling

Direct feedback for past performance. What happened, what was the impact, what should change.

• Performance reviews • After-action discussions • Behavioral feedback
Present

Coaching

In the moment. Real-time guidance as situations unfold. Helping them navigate what's happening right now.

• Real-time guidance • Decision support • Situational adjustment
Future

Mentoring

Growing a future leader. Long-term development toward who they could become.

• Career trajectory • Capability building • Identity formation

"There's a way to make somebody understand what their weaknesses are and how to apply themselves to overcome that—if they so choose. They have to make the choice. But do so in a way that's constructive."

06

The Correction Spectrum

Most folks, because it's easy, default to a somewhat destructive way of correcting behavior—either through commission or omission.

Destructive: Commission

Being an Asshole

Active harm. Demeaning. Public humiliation. Using position to diminish rather than develop.

Constructive

The Middle Path

Direct, clear, context-aware. Addresses the behavior while maintaining dignity. Creates path forward.

THIS IS THE TARGET
Destructive: Omission

Not Addressing It

Passive harm. Avoiding discomfort. Letting standards erode. The behavior continues, unchecked.

07

Lead Yourself First

A good leader ultimately has to figure out how to lead themselves.

There's a parallel between how you choose to lead externally and how you are actually speaking to yourself. The discipline to be consistent starts with self-discipline.

Case Study: Nick Saban

"He's very disciplined. He's disciplined with himself. You know what he's gonna do every hour of the day. He's so consistent."

The discipline to be consistent—and that comes back to yourself. Modeling behavior means you yourself have to be able to do it.

08

The Outcome

Good leadership coheres.
Bad leadership fractures.

Good Leadership Creates

  • → People stay because they believe in you
  • → They believe in the organization
  • → A stable of high-performing people
  • → People run through doors for you

Bad Leadership Creates

  • → High turnover
  • → Loss of trust
  • → Dissatisfaction from lack of human connection
  • → People run for the doors
The Proof

"At Fort Bragg, one of my excellent commanders was Jeff Colt, retired Major General now. People would run through doors for that guy."

"He left, and the new commander was not as good. He had big shoes to fill, but this guy was just totally off. People ran for the doors."

"I had the opportunity to stay—they asked me to stay—and I got orders to go, and I was like, 'See ya.' The whole reason was because of that new command team. I didn't trust them, and it wasn't a place I wanted to be."

09

The Ultimate Purpose

The purpose of good leadership is to inculcate a culture within whatever context that allows people to own it themselves.

To participate. To say: "That parking lot is my parking lot, as much as it's my comrade's parking lot, my team member's parking lot."

The leadership role is really to understand the human element more so than anything else—by being relatable as a human, but also understanding that humans respond in different ways to different types of interactions, and tailoring your method to the individual.

10

Decision Prompts

Questions to ask yourself in leadership moments. Based on the fundamentals above.

Before Deciding

"Have I taken time to understand the full context—or am I defaulting to binary thinking?"

Before Correcting

"Am I about to be constructive—or am I defaulting to commission (harsh) or omission (avoidance)?"

Before Praising/Admonishing

"Is this praise that should be public—or correction that should be private?"

Before Developing

"Is this moment calling for counseling (past), coaching (present), or mentoring (future)?"

Before Asking More

"Am I willing to sacrifice alongside them—or am I asking from a position of exemption?"

Before Speaking

"Am I being sincere—or can they sniff out the insincerity in two seconds?"

11

Actionable Behaviors

01

Model the standard yourself

Don't walk by the trash. Hold yourself to the same expectations. Be the discipline you want to see.

02

Pause for context before judging

When something happens that requires your judgment, resist the binary. Ask: what's the full picture here?

03

Protect dignity in correction

When you need to address behavior, do it in private. Find the constructive middle between harsh and avoidant.

04

Amplify recognition publicly

When someone does something right, acknowledge it where others can see. Build culture through visible appreciation.

05

Share the sacrifice

If you're asking people to give, give alongside them. Flatten hierarchy through shared experience.

06

Tailor your approach to the individual

Understand that humans respond differently to different interactions. Read the person, not just the situation.

The Bottom Line

Leadership is not about position.
It's about human connection.
Sincerity. Context. Ownership.

"Good leadership coheres. Bad leadership fractures and disintegrates."
